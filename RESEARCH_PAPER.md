## Abstract

Coal-fired power plants are among the largest single-facility sources of CO2 emissions, yet in many regions — including India — there is no independent, satellite-derived, per-facility emissions estimate to cross-check reported figures. This project develops two complementary, currently unfused pipelines toward that goal. **Track A** is a small convolutional neural network that classifies 64×64 pixel satellite tiles as "coal power plant" or "not," built by incrementally fusing Sentinel-5P NO2, Sentinel-5P SO2, and VIIRS thermal channels, and stress-tested against confounding "hard negative" sources (cities, steel plants, highways). **Track B** is a physics-based Integrated Mass Enhancement (IME) mass-balance pipeline that converts NASA OCO-3 XCO2 column soundings into a per-plant emission-rate estimate (tons CO2/year) with propagated uncertainty, cross-checked against NO2 co-location and ERA5 wind alignment.

A central methodological contribution of this work is locating and using a genuine, independent ground-truth emissions source — something the project lacked for most of its development. India's Central Electricity Authority (CEA) publishes a **CO2 Baseline Database**: unlike the satellite-thermal-proxy-inferred Climate TRACE dataset (used throughout as a benchmark but explicitly barred as a training label, since it is itself an unvalidated estimator), the CEA database is bottom-up — computed from each plant's own reported fuel consumption, gross calorific value, and IPCC/national emission factors, the same accounting basis used for real Clean Development Mechanism carbon-credit determinations. All 30 of this project's candidate facilities matched the CEA database by name. Evaluated against this real ground truth (originally N=17, leave-one-out cross-validation, since extended to N=24 once the 9 newly-added candidates gained a Q estimate and an activity signal, §5.3.1b/d), Track B's raw physics estimate has a mean absolute error of 1.02 in log-ratio space (roughly a 2.75× typical factor of error) with a systematic underestimate bias and a 45.8% bracketing rate (11/24); a single-feature correction — now `hit_days`, the number of per-overpass wind-matched days, rather than the background XCO2 noise feature (`bg_std_ppm`) that was strongest at N=17, a feature-ranking change reported directly rather than smoothed over (§5.3.1d) — reduces this to an MAE of 0.933, an ~8% improvement that is directionally consistent (positive in all 24 leave-one-facility-out re-fits) but nonetheless **not yet statistically significant at this sample size** (bootstrap 95% CI on the improvement: [−0.20, +0.38], includes zero) — both facts are reported together, since a point estimate without its confidence interval is not a complete scientific claim. Separately, benchmarked against Climate TRACE (24 facilities, extended from an original 17 once 6 newly-processed candidates gained a Q estimate), 9 (37.5%) of Track B's estimates fall within their own stated uncertainty bounds, a figure that itself moved from an initial, seasonally-biased 53% (N=17) after a later bug fix (month-stratifying the near/background comparison) dropped it to 35.3% (N=17), before the facility-count expansion brought it to its current 37.5% (N=24) — all numbers are reported, since they measure different things and neither supersedes the ground-truth-based result above. One facility, Rihand, disagrees with Climate TRACE by +134% despite scoring average-or-better than the 30-plant median on every input-quality metric available (overpass density, signal-to-noise, background-definition sensitivity, wind-match quality) — each was tested and ruled out as the cause, and the single-cause hunt is explicitly paused, not resolved. Two further benchmarking results, reported with the same honesty regardless of outcome, temper how all of the above should be read: against the simplest possible alternative, each plant's own nameplate capacity, Track B's satellite estimate loses outright (leave-one-out R²=0.527 for capacity alone versus R²=−0.152 for the satellite estimate, N=24, §5.2.1b) — the satellite approach's justification is independent verifiability, not superior accuracy, at this facility count; and a second physics-based Q estimator (Gaussian cross-sectional flux, benchmarked directly against the existing IME method) fit only 10 of 30 facilities and underperformed IME wherever a comparison was possible (§5.2.8), leaving IME the better-justified choice on direct comparative evidence rather than default alone. Separately, a regional context map pooling every OCO-3 sounding already collected across all 30 facilities finds only 8.8% of a 0.5°-gridded study region has enough coverage to report a value at all (§3.1) — a quantified statement of how sparse the underlying satellite data is outside each facility's own sampling footprint.

A second methodological contribution extends Track B toward the spatial and temporal outputs (a hotspot/density map, seasonal variation) that a scalar annual emission-rate estimate cannot provide. A wind-conditioned Gaussian plume model (Briggs/Pasquill-Gifford dispersion) was built and calibrated to Track B's mass-balance estimate, then validated — honestly, not just built and assumed correct — against the actual spatial pattern of real OCO-3 soundings using a permutation-test methodology (a random-sector null baseline, and a per-overpass wind-day-matched variant) across all 18 eligible facilities. **The result is a decisive negative one**: only 1 of 14 testable facilities shows a spatial alignment distinguishable from chance geometry, and it is marginal; per-overpass wind-day matching, the most likely proposed fix, does not rescue this and in fact reverses the one facility that had marginally survived. This is reported as a first-class finding, not suppressed: it demonstrates that a plausible-looking, physically-grounded spatial model can fail validation even when its calibrating scalar estimate is reasonable, and that only a permutation-based null test — not a raw significance test — can distinguish a real spatial effect from satellite-sampling-geometry artifacts. A related temporal extension (splitting the same soundings by calendar month) surfaced a second, independently verified structural finding: because the IME method's effective-plume-length term scales with the square root of sample count, monthly Q estimates from smaller subsamples are not comparable in absolute terms to the annual estimate, only to each other within a facility — a real property of the method, not a bug, worth stating explicitly given how easily it could be misread as genuine month-to-month emissions variation.

Track A's positive-class facility count was expanded from 4 physical sites to 20 mid-project, which resolved an earlier facility-level generalization collapse (plant recall on a single random held-out test set rose from 8% to 88%) — but a subsequent **exhaustive** leave-one-facility-out evaluation (21 folds, one per facility) showed that single-split figure was itself an artifact of an easy draw: true mean recall across all facilities was 47.2%, ranging from 0% to 100% by facility. A follow-up analysis found this per-facility variation was strongly predicted by the detector's own confidence on that facility (r=+0.90), suggesting the failure mode was intrinsic signal ambiguity rather than memorization. A direct follow-up experiment tested this: light data augmentation made no difference, but exporting a matching second year of satellite tiles for the under-represented facilities (closing a real data-quantity gap, not a synthetic one) raised true LOFO recall to **69.1%**, leaving only two facilities (Kudgi, ShriSingajiMalwa) at 0% recall — both since diagnosed as a genuine satellite-observability limit rather than a fixable modeling gap, a conclusion Grad-CAM's per-channel attribution independently corroborates (§5.6.1).

This paper documents the methodology, results, and honestly-reported findings — including a facility-count-limited reliability model that flipped from negative to positive once the facility set grew (N=7→17), and three plants showing unexplained negative CO2 enhancement, later resolved to noise or a seasonal-sampling artifact — produced over the course of this work, and lays out the specific gaps that remain before the two tracks can be fused into a single, validated estimation system.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

Independent verification of industrial CO2 emissions matters for climate policy, especially in regions where self-reported facility-level emissions data is sparse or unverified. Satellite remote sensing — NO2 and SO2 as combustion co-emitted tracers, thermal infrared as an activity proxy, and direct CO2 column measurement from missions like OCO-2/3 — offers, in principle, an independent channel for this kind of verification. This project targets India's largest coal-fired power plants as a test case, motivated in part by the observation (documented in this project's own `RESEARCH_PLAN.md`) that an India-specific per-facility benchmark of this kind does not appear to exist in the literature surveyed.

### 1.2 Problem Statement

Two related but distinct sub-problems are addressed:

1. **Detection:** given a satellite image tile, is there a coal power plant in it? This is useful both as a standalone monitoring tool and, potentially, as an independent activity signal that could inform or correct an emissions estimate.
2. **Quantification:** given satellite CO2 column measurements over a known plant location, what is the plant's annual CO2 emission rate, and with what uncertainty?

### 1.3 Contributions

- A reproducible, checkpointed, facility-CSV-driven pipeline (`process_plant.py`) that scans, downloads, and processes OCO-3 granules for an arbitrary Indian coal plant, computing a full IME-based emission-rate estimate with three-term propagated uncertainty.
- A CNN-based detector fused across three physically distinct satellite channels (NO2, SO2, VIIRS thermal), with an explicit, quantified demonstration of the difference between tile-level and facility-level evaluation — a methodological point with direct relevance to any similar satellite-tile classification study.
- A worked uncertainty-diagnosis case study (the "Talcher" plant) showing how a tight confidence interval can still be badly wrong, and identifying background-annulus-definition sensitivity as a previously unmodeled uncertainty source.
- A demonstration that a single random facility-level train/test split, while methodologically correct in expectation, can itself substantially overstate real generalization at small facility counts: an **exhaustive** leave-one-facility-out harness (`lofo_track_a.py`) built after expanding the positive class to 20 facilities shows true mean recall (47.2%) is roughly half the single-split figure (88%) it superseded.
- A feasibility experiment predicting the emission estimator's own uncertainty from an independent activity signal, first reported as an honest negative result at N=7 facilities, then re-run and found positive (LOO R²=0.212) once the facility set grew to N=17 — illustrating how a facility-count-limited negative result should be revisited, not treated as final, once more data becomes available.
- A working benchmark of the estimator's output against Climate TRACE for the Indian power sector across 17 facilities — with results, including a recalibrated (and less flattering) uncertainty-bracketing rate at the larger sample size, stated explicitly.
- Identification of an independent, non-satellite-inferred ground-truth emissions source for Indian coal plants (CEA's CO2 Baseline Database, bottom-up from reported fuel consumption) — matched to all 30 candidate facilities — and the project's first genuine, leave-one-out cross-validated Q-correcting model built against it, reducing estimator error (MAE 1.01→0.902 in log-ratio space) using a single, physically interpretable feature, reported with a bootstrap confidence interval rather than only a point estimate.
- A wind-conditioned Gaussian plume (Briggs/Pasquill-Gifford) hotspot/density-map model, calibrated to Track B's mass-balance estimate — apparently absent from both Climate TRACE (no published per-pixel output) and this project's cited baseline — together with a permutation-test validation methodology (random-sector and wind-day-matched null baselines) that correctly identifies when an apparent spatial effect is indistinguishable from satellite-sampling-geometry artifacts, demonstrated across 18 facilities.
- A verified structural finding for IME-based emission-rate estimation at sub-annual time resolution: the effective-plume-length term's square-root dependence on sample count means naively-computed monthly estimates are not comparable in absolute terms to an annual estimate from the same method — a property of the method worth stating explicitly for any future work attempting sub-annual IME estimates, not specific to this project's facilities.
- A machine-readable export schema (JSON Schema draft 2020-12) for downstream integration, designed so that no estimated quantity can be read without its validation status attached (e.g. the plume map's `validated` field is fixed `false`, with the reason given inline) — intended to prevent exactly the kind of overclaiming this paper's own honesty discipline is built around, at the data-interchange level rather than only in prose.
- An explainability layer closing a gap earlier stages of this project left open: Grad-CAM extended from the original single-channel detector to the 3-channel (NO2+SO2+VIIRS) architecture actually used for the paper's LOFO results, with a new per-channel attribution capability, plus SHAP feature attribution for the CEA correction model — which surfaced and reported, rather than reconciled away, a disagreement between SHAP's multivariate ranking and the project's earlier single-feature correlation ranking (§5.6).
- A systematic elimination study on a single anomalous facility (Rihand, +134% Climate TRACE error despite good inputs): four candidate explanations, each tested both as a general N=24 predictor and as a Rihand-specific check, all ruled out — reported as a paused investigation rather than a false resolution (§5.2.7).
- A direct benchmark against the simplest possible non-satellite alternative: each plant's own nameplate capacity beats Track B's physics-based estimate outright at predicting CEA ground truth (LOO R² 0.527 vs. −0.152, N=24), a gap that holds even on IME's own best-case subset — reported as a genuine limit on the satellite approach's current accuracy, reframing its justification as independent verifiability rather than superior accuracy (§5.2.1b).
- A second Q-estimation method (Gaussian cross-sectional flux) benchmarked directly against IME across all 30 facilities — not merely proposed: it fit only 10, and underperformed IME on CEA ground truth wherever the two were comparable (LOO R² −0.966 vs. −0.111), giving IME's status as this project's estimator direct comparative support rather than default-choice justification (§5.2.8).
- A regional XCO2 context map pooling all 30 facilities' already-downloaded OCO-3 soundings into a single 0.5°-gridded coverage picture, quantifying — not just illustrating — how sparse the region's usable satellite coverage actually is (130/1,470 cells, 8.8%) (§3.1).

### 1.4 Scope and Framing

The two tracks (detection and quantification) are developed and validated **separately** in this work. A one-directional bridge exists — the trained detector is used purely for inference to produce an "activity signal" for Track B's facilities — but no joint or corrective model has yet been trained combining the two. This is a deliberate scope decision, not an oversight: `RESEARCH_PLAN.md` explicitly instructs against retraining the detector prematurely, and the fusion step is treated as a distinct, still-open piece of future work (§8).

### 3.1 Regional XCO2 Coverage: A Context Map, Not a Product

`regional_xco2_map.py` pools every OCO-3 sounding already downloaded for all 30 facilities (52,898 soundings total, no new download) into a single regional dataset and bins it into a 0.5° grid over the study region — the 30 facilities' own lat/lon extent (14.70–29.92°N, 69.55–88.10°E) plus a 1.0° margin, rounded outward to the nearest 0.5° line (13.5–31.0°N, 68.5–89.5°E; 35×42 = 1,470 possible cells). This is explicitly descriptive, not a new estimator: it answers "what does background CO2 look like across the region," in the same spirit as the regional-XCO2-context literature (e.g. STEL, DNN-CAMS), for this paper's own data-coverage picture — it is not used anywhere as an input to Track A or Track B.

Per-cell mean XCO2 is reported only for cells with at least 3 soundings; cells with 1–2 soundings are masked identically to empty cells (not treated as a low-confidence zero), a threshold recorded directly in `data/regional_xco2_grid.json`'s `n_soundings_min_required` field rather than left as a silent default. Applying that floor, **only 130 of 1,470 cells (8.8%) have enough coverage to report a value** — the region's real signal here is coverage sparsity itself: most of the study area has no usable OCO-3 data at this resolution, and what coverage exists is concentrated almost entirely around the 30 facilities' own near-plant/background sampling zones (§5.2.1 reports the same sparsity story at facility granularity — sounding counts per facility ranging from 1 to over 7,000 — this section shows it is also a spatial, not just per-facility, property of the dataset). Where cells do have enough coverage, mean XCO2 ranges roughly 410–419 ppm regionally, consistent with typical background dry-air XCO2 variation rather than any per-cell anomaly. Figure: `data/regional_xco2_map.png` (heatmap, with the 30 facility locations overlaid, sized and colored by CEA absolute emissions for context); full binned values in `data/regional_xco2_grid.json`.

## 4. Methods

### 4.1 Track A: Satellite-Tile Plant Detector

**Architecture** (constant across all channel-count variants):

```
Conv(C_in → 16) → BatchNorm → SiLU → MaxPool
Conv(16 → 32)   → BatchNorm → SiLU → MaxPool
Conv(32 → 64)   → BatchNorm → SiLU
GlobalAvgPool
Dropout(0.3)
Linear(64 → 2)
```

Trained with AdamW (learning rate 3×10⁻⁴) for 30 epochs under cross-entropy loss. `C_in` varies with the channel-fusion stage: 1 (NO2 only), 2 (+SO2), or 3 (+VIIRS).

**Channel fusion strategy.** Channels are fused incrementally rather than jointly from the start, so that each addition's marginal effect on accuracy and specific failure modes can be isolated. NO2 was chosen as the base channel for its direct link to combustion; SO2 was added specifically to address confusion with cities (motor-vehicle NO2 sources without matching SO2); VIIRS thermal was added to address confusion with other industrial heat sources not distinguished by the gas channels alone.

**Hard-negative curriculum.** Beyond simple rural "negative" tiles, a curated set of confounding locations — cities, steel manufacturing plants, and highway corridors — was constructed and validated with a minimum-distance-from-any-known-plant check (≥80 km, haversine). This set was expanded over the course of the project (5 → 10 highway locations) specifically to probe and reduce a persistent highway-related confusion mode.

**Train/test split methodology — the central methodological finding of Track A.** Two splitting strategies were implemented and directly compared:
- *Tile-level split*: individual tiles assigned to train/test at random, regardless of which physical facility or which month they represent.
- *Facility-level split*: all tiles belonging to a given physical site are kept together on one side of the split, stratified by class (necessary since the positive class spans only 5 distinct facilities).

The facility-level split is the methodologically correct one for measuring genuine generalization to unseen plants; the tile-level split risks (and, as shown in §5.1, did in fact produce) leakage, since the same facility's signature can appear in both train and test simply under a different month's tile.

### 4.2 Track B: Physics-Based CO2 Emission-Rate Estimation

**Pipeline.** For a given plant, `process_plant.py` (i) searches and downloads matching OCO-3 granules for the target year via `earthaccess`, with checkpointing so an interrupted run resumes rather than restarting; (ii) filters soundings to quality flag 0 within a tight radius of the plant; (iii) computes mean XCO2 in a near-plant zone (<0.25°) versus a background ring (0.4°–0.9°), and their difference as the "CO2 enhancement"; (iv) performs an independent NO2 co-location check (distance from the plant coordinate to the peak-NO2 pixel) and a wind-alignment check (does the CO2 enhancement sit downwind of the plant, per ERA5?) as physical plausibility signals; and (v) appends results to a shared results file, using file locking to guard against concurrent-process corruption.

**Emission-rate formula.** Despite the module name (`physics_ime.py`), this is explicitly not a ground-level Gaussian plume model — OCO-3 XCO2 is a column-averaged quantity, not a surface concentration, so ground-level dispersion physics does not directly apply. Instead, the Integrated Mass Enhancement (IME) method (Varon et al. 2018; Nassar et al. 2017; Reuter et al. 2019) is used:

```
Q = U_eff · IME / L_eff
```

- `IME` (kg): total excess column-mass CO2 over near-plant soundings. Computed by converting the ppm excess to kg/m² (standard surface pressure 101,325 Pa; CO2/air molar-mass ratio 0.04401/0.02897), then scaling by an assumed 2.25 km² footprint area per OCO-3 sounding.
- `L_eff` (m): effective plume length, taken as the square root of the covered plume area.
- `U_eff`: effective wind speed, `U_eff = α · wind_speed` with α = 0.5 (the Varon et al. 2018 default, correcting a 10 m surface wind measurement toward the deeper-layer mixing wind relevant to plume transport).

**Uncertainty model.** Three independent relative-uncertainty terms are combined in quadrature into a single `q_rel_std`:
1. *Wind term*: relative standard deviation of wind speed matched to actual sounding dates where sufficient per-overpass data exists (falling back to an annual-mean estimate otherwise), deduplicated to unique days.
2. *IME sampling term*: 500-resample bootstrap over the near-plant and background sounding populations.
3. *Background-definition term*: IME recomputed under 5 alternative definitions of the background annulus, holding the near-plant zone fixed — added specifically in response to a diagnosed failure case (§5.2.3).

Empirically, the wind term dominates the total uncertainty budget in most facilities examined (45–59% relative standard deviation, versus 4–6% for IME sampling), consistent with the wind-error-dominance finding noted in the literature review (§2.1).

### 4.3 Spatial Extension: A Wind-Conditioned Gaussian Plume Hotspot Model

Neither Track A nor Track B, as described above, produces a spatial output — Track A classifies whole tiles, and Track B's near/background comparison is a single scalar circle-versus-annulus test with no directional component. To address this gap directly, a ground-level Gaussian plume model was added, calibrated to Track B's mass-balance estimate but architecturally separate from it, following the standard continuous point-source formulation (Pasquill-Gifford; Briggs 1973 rural dispersion-coefficient tables, as tabulated in Turner's *Workbook of Atmospheric Dispersion Estimates*):

```
C(x, y, 0) = Q / (π · U · σy(x) · σz(x)) · exp(−y² / (2σy(x)²)) · exp(−H² / (2σz(x)²))
```

where `x`/`y` are downwind/crosswind distance from the source, `U` is wind speed, `H` is effective stack height, and `σy(x)`/`σz(x)` are the Briggs dispersion coefficients for a given Pasquill stability class (A–F). `Q` is taken directly from Track B's *raw* (not ground-truth-corrected) estimate — the correction model is a statistical benchmarking adjustment, not a re-derivation of the physical mass flux, and conflating the two would misrepresent what physically flows through this equation.

**Parameterization, stated as explicit assumptions, not hidden defaults.** Stability class is fixed at class B (moderately unstable), justified by this project's observed per-overpass wind speeds (~1.2–1.8 m/s across the prototype facilities, consistent with light-wind daytime conditions) and OCO-3's typical daytime overpass timing — not derived from a full insolation/cloud-cover scheme, and tested via an explicit stability-class × stack-height sensitivity ablation (§5.4). Effective stack height defaults to 220 m, citing India's CPCB/MoEFCC regulatory stack-height norm for coal-fired thermal plants ≥500 MW aggregate capacity (every facility in this study exceeds that threshold by a wide margin), again with an explicit sensitivity ablation (150/220/275 m) rather than a claimed-precise value.

**A wind-direction-convention pitfall, documented because it is easy to reproduce.** This project's own `wind_deg` field (from the Track B pipeline) encodes the direction the wind blows *toward*, not the standard meteorological *from* convention the plume equation expects. Verifying this before running the model — rather than after — avoided every plume silently pointing 180° in the wrong direction without raising an error.

**Explicit honesty constraint carried through to every downstream use of this model.** OCO-3's sparse per-sounding footprint (~1.6 km, non-daily revisit) cannot support a claim that this map is pixel-accurate. It is a physics-consistent *visualization* calibrated to an independently-derived total mass flux, not a directly observed spatial measurement — §5.4 reports a dedicated validation attempt against this exact claim, honestly.

### 4.4 Temporal Extension: Monthly Emission-Rate Estimates

All Track B OCO-3 soundings are drawn from calendar year 2020 only (a fixed search range in the ingestion pipeline), so a monthly time series is a genuine 12-point split of already-collected data, not a new acquisition. Each calendar month's near-plant and background soundings are passed through the identical IME formula used for the annual estimate (§4.2), independently per month, subject to the same minimum-sample threshold (5 near-plant and 5 background soundings) the annual pipeline already enforces — most facilities therefore produce a sparse, not a full 12-point, series. Wind speed is held at the same annual/overpass-aggregated value for every month (a documented simplification, not a per-month wind estimate — see §5.5's discussion of what this does and does not permit); uncertainty is computed from the IME-sampling bootstrap term only, not the full three-term annual budget.

### 4.5 Explainability: Grad-CAM and SHAP

Two explainability layers were added on top of Track A and the CEA correction model, closing the gap earlier drafts of this paper flagged as open.

**Grad-CAM, extended to the 3-channel detector.** The project's original Grad-CAM script supported only the earliest, NO2-only single-channel detector; it was never extended to `Detector3`, the NO2+SO2+VIIRS architecture actually used for the paper's headline LOFO results (§5.1). `gradcam_3channel.py` adapts the same hook-based approach (forward/backward hooks on the last convolutional layer, gradient-weighted channel-activation sum, ReLU, bilinear upsample to tile resolution) to `Detector3`, using the facility-split checkpoint (`detector3_2ch_mixed_facility_split.pt`) whose LOFO behavior the paper reports on. Because the input tensor carries three physically distinct channels, this extension adds a diagnostic the single-channel version could not offer: per-channel attribution, computed as input×gradient saliency summed per channel and normalized to shares — which channel (NO2, SO2, or VIIRS) actually drove a given prediction, not just where in the tile the model looked.

**SHAP, added to the CEA correction model.** The correction model's feature analysis (§5.3.1b–c) previously went no further than single-feature Pearson correlation and a 2-feature LOO-MAE comparison — informative, but not a proper attribution method. `shap_correction_model.py` fits a linear model (`sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression`) on standardized versions of the same 8 candidate features, over the full N=17 facility set, and explains it with `shap.LinearExplainer`. A linear model was chosen deliberately over a tree ensemble: `RESEARCH_PLAN.md` §8's caution against complex multi-feature models at this facility count applies to the explainer's underlying model as much as to the correction model itself. This fit is *not* leave-one-out cross-validated — SHAP explains a fixed model's own predictions, not a generalization estimate, so the LOO-CV MAE figures already reported in §5.3.1b–c remain this project's actual generalization claim; SHAP is an attribution layer on top, not a replacement for it.

### 5.1 Track A: Detector Accuracy, the Facility-Split Finding, and Exhaustive LOFO

The tile-level-vs-facility-level gap (81.2%→67.3%, on an otherwise identical model and dataset) remains the paper's first quantitative demonstration that tile-level evaluation overstates generalization. But the facility-count expansion adds a second, sharper methodological point: even a *facility-level* split can mislead at small facility counts, because a single random draw of held-out sites may happen to be unrepresentative. Expanding the positive class from 4 to 20 facilities and retraining resolved the original recall collapse (8%→88%), which at first reads as confirmation that more data fixed the generalization problem. It was only the subsequent exhaustive leave-one-facility-out evaluation — training 21 fresh models, each with exactly one facility's tiles excluded — that revealed the 88% figure itself depended on which 4 facilities happened to be excluded in that one draw. True recall, averaged honestly across every facility rather than one lucky subset, is 47.2% (tile-weighted: 48.7%), with a wide spread: Anpara, Korba, Rihand, Talcher, Vindhyachal, and Sasan all generalize well (recall ≥0.83), while Kahalgaon, Kudgi, and Mouda generalize essentially not at all (recall = 0.00). A follow-up correlation analysis (`lofo_recall_correlates.py`, N=20) found this per-facility variation is strongly predicted by the standard (non-held-out) model's own confidence on that facility (activity_prob_mean, r=+0.903, leave-one-out R²=0.783) — the facilities the model generalizes to are exactly the ones with a clear, unambiguous NO2+SO2+VIIRS combustion signature even when it hasn't seen them, suggesting the failure mode is intrinsic signal ambiguity per facility rather than memorization of specific training sites. Grad-CAM visualization of the earliest (NO2-only) model's attention supports the leakage conclusion qualitatively: the model attends to any sufficiently compact, sufficiently intense NO2 hotspot, consistent with a generic "concentrated combustion" detector rather than a coal-plant-specific one.

**5.1.1 Closing most of the LOFO gap: a data-quantity fix, not augmentation.** The 47.2% LOFO figure invited two competing explanations: a genuine per-facility signal-strength ceiling (supported by §5.3's activity-confidence correlation), or a training-diversity artifact, since the 16 newly-added facilities had only 12 tiles each (2020 only) versus 24 for the original top-5 (2019+2020). Two follow-up experiments distinguished between them. First, light spatial augmentation (random flip/90°-rotation per training epoch) was applied to the same 21-fold LOFO harness with no new data — mean recall was essentially unchanged (47.2%→46.8%, tile-weighted 48.7%→48.7%), ruling out training-set diversity as the primary driver. Second, a matching second data year (2019) was exported for the 16 facilities, bringing every facility to the same 24-tile depth as the original top-5, and LOFO was re-run (22 folds, the extra fold being an incidentally-included unrelated facility). **Mean recall rose to 69.1% (tile-weighted 70.5%)** — 17 of 21 comparable facilities improved, several sharply. Augmentation was then reapplied on top of this richer dataset and again made results slightly worse, not better (69.1%→67.2%). The conclusion is that the original LOFO gap was substantially a real data-quantity limitation rather than a fixed signal-strength ceiling: two facilities, **Kudgi and ShriSingajiMalwa**, still show 0% LOFO recall after the fix, narrowing the open generalization weakness from roughly half the facility set to these two specific cases. A targeted follow-up diagnosis of these two facilities found their raw NO2/SO2 tile intensity sits at or below the rural-negative-class noise floor in every individual month, roughly 3–4x below the rest of the positive class, and ruled out a coordinate/tile-placement bug (their NO2-peak-offset distances are smaller, not larger, than several facilities the detector recognizes perfectly). This is a genuine satellite-observability limit rather than a fixable modeling gap: no architecture or training-regime change would be expected to help, since the discriminating signal is not present in the tile data itself.

**5.2.1b Capacity Baseline: A Sobering Comparison.** Every result in this section is measured against Climate TRACE or CEA — never against the simplest possible alternative: does this project's satellite estimate actually beat just using each plant's nameplate capacity? `baseline_capacity.py` asks that directly, using the same leave-one-out log-space methodology as §5.2.8 and §5.3.1b–d, across four predictors of CEA ground truth at the full N=24: (A) `capacity_mw` alone, (B) IME Q ungated, (C) IME Q restricted to the "quality gate" subset (`hit_days≥10`, `wind_co2_diff_deg≤60`, N=7), and (D) capacity and IME Q combined. Capacity alone wins outright: LOO R²=0.527 (MAE(log)=0.398, within-2x 20/24) versus IME's R²=−0.152 (MAE=0.596, within-2x 15/24) — and combining the two does not recover IME's shortfall (R²=0.505, marginally *worse* than capacity alone). The gap holds even on IME's best-case subset: restricted to the same 7 gated plants, capacity-only still leads (R²=0.665) over gated IME (R²=−0.242).

This is a genuine, not a rhetorical, negative result for the satellite approach on raw predictive accuracy, and it reframes how §5.2.7's Rihand story should be read: a plant whose capacity alone would have placed its emissions in a defensible range still produced a satellite estimate 134% off. The honest justification for continuing the satellite work is therefore not "more accurate than capacity" — on this evidence, at N=24, it is not — but that capacity is nameplate rating, not measured output, and works here only because these particular plants run near rated capacity most of the year; it offers no path to independent verification in a geography without CEA-grade self-reported data, which is the one thing satellite estimation can offer regardless of accuracy today. Full four-way and gated-subset comparison: `data/baseline_capacity_results.json`.

**5.2.2 Example estimates.** For Sasan, the full pipeline (per-overpass wind, three-term uncertainty) produces an estimate of approximately 40.0 million tons CO2/year, with an uncertainty band of roughly ±18.1 million tons (dominated by the wind term at ~44.6% relative contribution, versus ~4.1% for IME sampling and ~6.5% for background definition). An earlier, less-developed version of the pipeline (using a flawed annual-mean wind average, see §6) had produced substantially different point estimates for the same facilities before the wind-averaging methodology was corrected — a roughly threefold change in the resulting estimate for at least two facilities examined.

**5.2.3 Uncertainty case study: Talcher.** One facility produced an internally puzzling result: the *tightest* uncertainty interval of any facility examined, yet the estimate that most disagreed with the Climate TRACE benchmark (roughly half the benchmark value). A targeted diagnosis found the facility's raw CO2 enhancement had an unusually low signal-to-noise ratio (0.18, versus 1.27 for a comparably-sized, well-bracketed facility) and that its IME estimate was highly sensitive to how the background annulus was defined (a ~20% swing across 5 reasonable definitions, versus ~4% for the comparison facility). This directly motivated adding the background-definition uncertainty term described in §4.2, closing a real gap in the uncertainty model that the two originally-implemented terms (wind, IME sampling) did not capture.

**5.2.4 Climate TRACE benchmark.** Bracketing rate — the share of facilities whose Track B estimate falls within its own stated uncertainty interval against Climate TRACE — moved through four honest revisions as the pipeline matured, each a genuinely better-measured number, not a regression: 5/7 (71%) at the original small facility count; 9/17 (53%) once the sample grew large enough to surface new misses (Kahalgaon, Talcher, RGundem, Korba, ShriSingajiMalwa, Tamnar, Sasan, Tirora); 6/17 (35.3%) after §5.2.6's month-stratification bug fix; and **9/24 (37.5%)**, its current value, after `pull_climate_trace.py` was re-run against the 9 newly-added candidates (§5.2.1) matched by nearest-centroid (`data/climate_trace_comparison.json`). Of those 9, 6 have both a Q estimate and a Climate TRACE match (the other 3 — Lalitpur, Akaltara, Bellary — have no Track B estimate, the same low-coverage exclusion as Mundra/Sipat/Simhadri); 3 of the 6 are bracketed (KGudemNew 1.47×, Pryagraj(Bara) 1.92×, RayalSeema 0.91×) and 3 are not (Dadri(Nctpp) 3.70× overestimate, Raichur 0.36× and Sagardighi 0.22× underestimates). The final step (35.3%→37.5%) is small enough — 6 facilities — to read as noise around a stable rate rather than a trend, the same small-sample caution that applies to the earlier steps. Both directions of mismatch (over- and under-estimate) are reported as findings requiring further investigation, not proof that Climate TRACE is correct and Track B wrong or vice versa — see §2.2 and §6. Full facility-level comparison, predicted-vs-actual, and residual-ratio figures (pre-§5.2.6 fix, pre-facility-expansion) are in `data/eval_climate_trace_comparison.png`.

**5.2.6 Month-stratifying the near/background comparison: a real bug, a worse-looking number.** The ShriSingajiMalwa investigation (§5.2.5) surfaced a general gap: `physics_ime.py` did not stratify its near-plant/background comparison by month, so any facility with uneven near/background month coverage was exposed to the same seasonal-sampling-imbalance artifact. Fixed by restricting the background population (both the main annulus and each of the 5 alternate sensitivity-term definitions) to only the months the near-plant zone was actually sampled in, falling back to the unrestricted background when day data is unavailable or stratifying would leave too few soundings. Re-running for all 20 facilities changed several point estimates substantially — ShriSingajiMalwa +320%, Chhabra −93%, Tirora −90% — confirming the seasonal bias was not isolated to the one facility that originally surfaced it. Re-running the Climate TRACE comparison at the same N=17 found **calibration dropped further, to 35.3% (6/17)**, reported honestly rather than treated as a sign the fix was wrong: several facilities' month-restricted background sample sizes fell sharply (e.g. Tirora 524→127 soundings, RGundem 80→61), trading a systematic seasonal bias for increased sampling noise — itself a new, explicit caveat rather than evidence the underlying physics changed for the worse. This illustrates the same lesson as the tile-level-vs-facility-level Track A finding (§5.1): fixing a real methodological bug does not guarantee a better-looking external validation number, especially against an already-imperfect benchmark.

**5.2.5 Root-causing the negative-CO2-enhancement anomaly.** Three facilities (ShriSingajiMalwa, Koradi, Tamnar) produced a *negative* near-minus-background CO2 enhancement — physically implausible for an operating combustion source. A dedicated diagnosis (`diagnose_negative_enhancement.py`, extending the Talcher methodology with a statistical-significance check on the near-minus-background difference) found that **2 of the 3 are not genuinely negative at all**: Koradi (z=−1.58) and Tamnar (z=−1.33) are both statistically consistent with zero given the standard error of the difference — the correct characterization is "no detectable enhancement given available signal-to-noise," not a real negative signal. This resolves the apparent contradiction with the Talcher-derived heuristic that good wind alignment implies a trustworthy signal: Tamnar's good wind/CO2-offset alignment (16°, the best of the three) correctly says its NO2 plume geometry is plausible, but wind alignment and CO2 signal-to-noise are computed from entirely independent data, so a facility can have both a physically sensible wind alignment and a CO2 signal too weak to measure — the two diagnostics are orthogonal, not in tension. ShriSingajiMalwa initially looked different: its negative enhancement (−1.052 ppm, z=−5.32) was statistically significant, not noise. A dedicated follow-up (`diagnose_shrisingajimalwa.py`) resolved this too, by testing a seasonal-sampling confound the original diagnosis had not checked: all 59 of ShriSingajiMalwa's near-plant soundings come from a single month (January), while its background ring also draws from April/May (mean XCO2 approximately 415.6–416.5 ppm, roughly 4 ppm above January's approximately 412 ppm baseline for reasons unrelated to the plant — a real seasonal atmospheric-CO2 cycle) and October, likely because OCO-3's swath geometry only crossed the tight near-plant circle on some overpasses. Restricting the near-vs-background comparison to the one month both zones actually share (January) flips the sign: near = 412.321 ppm versus background = 412.029 ppm, a small positive difference (+0.292 ppm, z=2.13) consistent with a real, weak plant signal rather than a negative anomaly. **All three originally-flagged facilities are therefore resolved**: none represents a real negative CO2 signal or a pipeline bug. All three also show elevated background-definition sensitivity relative to the well-bracketed Rihand comparison facility, consistent with the same thin-signal fragility pattern the Talcher case study identified. One general methodological gap surfaced by this diagnosis remains: `physics_ime.py` does not currently stratify its near/background comparison by month, so the same seasonal-sampling-imbalance failure mode could in principle recur for any facility with uneven near/background month coverage.

**5.2.7 Rihand case study: four explanations tested, none sufficient.** Rihand's Q estimate disagrees with Climate TRACE by +134% despite scoring well on every input-quality metric already in the pipeline — an anomaly distinct from Talcher's (§5.2.3), where the tight-interval mismatch at least correlated with low SNR and high background sensitivity. §5.2.1b's capacity-baseline comparison sharpens why this specific miss matters: Rihand's own nameplate capacity would have placed its emissions in a far more defensible range than the satellite estimate did. Four candidate explanations were tested, each in two ways: (a) as a predictor of `|log_ratio|` across the full N=24 feature table, and (b) as a specific check of whether it explains Rihand.

*Overpass density.* Subsampling Vindhyachal, Rihand, and Sasan (16–19 available days) down to 3 days, 200 repeats per step, found Vindhyachal and Sasan degrade smoothly as coverage drops — density is a real effect for them — but Rihand is already +134% off at its *best* available coverage (15–16 days), ruling out thin coverage as its cause. An ascending-threshold version of this scan was initially fooled by a lucky noisy draw at low `n_days`; fixed to require an unbroken stable run down from full coverage before being trusted. At `n_days≥6`, 17 of 24 plants clear a stability bar.

*Signal-to-noise.* Computed as (near-plant mean − background) / background std for all 30 facilities. Rihand's SNR is 1.27, above the 30-plant median (0.36) — the opposite of a weak-signal problem.

*Background-definition sensitivity.* Rihand's swing across the same 5 background-annulus definitions used in §5.2.3 is 4.3%, confirmed identical to the original 2-plant check, far below Talcher's known-bad 20.2% reference — not a background-definition problem either.

*Wind-match quality.* The real (unthresholded) per-overpass wind-match rate, computed for all 30 plants, required a small additive fix to `physics_ime.py` (`n_wind_days_raw_matched`, `wind_days_matched_speeds`; behavior-preserving, verified against existing results before use). Rihand's rate is 25% (4 of 16 days), the 71st percentile — again above median, not below.

**Synthesis.** Ranked by |r| against `|log_ratio|` at N=24: wind-match rate (−0.328) > hit_days (−0.310, §5.2 baseline) > SNR (+0.269) > background swing% (+0.161). Wind-match rate is nominally strongest, but the margin over the already-known `hit_days` predictor is small, and none of the four explains more than ~11% of the variance. This is not one real signal buried under three weak ones — all four are individually weak, and Rihand scores average-or-better on every one of them despite its large error. The single-cause hunt on Rihand is paused here, not resolved; §7 and §8 list what remains untried. This suggests point-source estimation failure is not always attributable to a single measurable cause, which has implications for any correction model trained on these diagnostics. Full per-plant results and plots: `data/overpass_density_results.json`, `data/snr_all_plants.json`, `data/bg_sensitivity_all_plants.json`, `data/wind_match_quality_all_plants.json`.

**5.2.8 A Gaussian Cross-Sectional Alternative: Succeeds on One-Third of Facilities, Underperforms IME Where Comparable.** IME (§4.2) has been this project's only Q estimator by default, never benchmarked against an alternative. `physics_gaussian_crosssection.py` tests one: it rotates each facility's near-plant soundings into a downwind/crosswind frame using the facility's annual-mean wind direction, keeps only the downwind half, and fits a Gaussian to the crosswind excess-mass profile — integrating that fit analytically gives flux directly (Q = U_eff·A·σ·√2π), the "cross-sectional flux" method referenced alongside IME in the point-source literature (e.g. Varon et al. 2018 for CH4), using the plume's actual spatial shape rather than IME's direction-agnostic near-plant disk. The first run produced physically impossible fits (σ in the tens of thousands of km) for 7 facilities, traced to `curve_fit` degenerating toward its unbounded limit on noisy, largely flat downwind data; fixed by capping σ at 3× the near-plant disk radius and treating a fit pinned at that cap as degenerate rather than real — the same kind of self-caught methodology bug §5.2.7 logged for the overpass-density experiment, and worth naming here for the same reason.

**Per-overpass wind direction, tested (Week 18): closes nothing.** The annual-mean-direction limitation above was named, not fixed, when this section was first written. `physics_gaussian_crosssection_perOverpassWind.py` closes it directly: rerunning the 18 facilities with a cached per-overpass wind-direction series (`data/daily_wind/`), rotating each near-plant sounding by its OWN overpass day's direction instead of one plant-wide annual angle. Fit rate barely moved (9/18 vs. 8/18 among these same 18 facilities under the annual mean) and churned rather than improved — 3 facilities newly fit (Anpara, ChandrapurCoal, Koradi), 2 newly failed (Farakka, Korba). Of the 6 facilities that fit under both methods, only 1 (Kudgi) moved closer to CEA truth; the other 5 moved further, most sharply Sasan (|log ratio| 0.131→1.226, from this project's best-performing Gaussian estimate to one of its worst). LOO R² against CEA on the shared 6-facility subset got *worse* under per-overpass direction (−0.212→−0.447), and IME still wins decisively on the full 9-facility per-overpass set (R²=0.115 vs −0.447). The likely mechanism: per-sounding rotation — each sounding rotated by its own day's actual, often widely different, wind bearing — fragments the usable sample rather than sharpening it; `n_overpass_days_used` collapsed to 3–5 days per facility under per-overpass rotation, since only sounding-days whose actual wind happened to point that specific sounding into its own downwind half count at all, versus the annual run's fuller, consistently-defined downwind half. **Verdict unchanged and now closed, not just untested**: wind-direction resolution was not the fixable half of this method's negative result — the weakness is more fundamental, most likely OCO-3's sparse per-overpass sampling itself, exactly as first suspected. Full comparison: `data/gaussian_crosssection_perOverpassWind_results.json`, `WEEK18_LOG.txt`.

Run across all 30 facilities, the method fit only **10 of 30** — the other 20 skipped for too few downwind soundings or the degenerate-fit cap above, meaning two-thirds of the facility set produced no comparison at all, not a worse one. On the 10 facilities where both methods produced a Q, the two are positively but loosely related (r=0.640 in log space; per-facility IME/Gaussian ratios range 0.13–1.33). Where a direct comparison is possible, IME wins: leave-one-out R² against CEA ground truth (same methodology as `baseline_capacity.py`) is −0.111 for IME versus **−0.966 for Gaussian cross-section**, on the identical N=10 subset. Rihand's Gaussian estimate lands marginally closer to CEA than IME's (42.9M vs 48.3M t/yr against a CEA truth of 20.6M) but remains roughly 2× off — not a resolution of §5.2.7's still-paused Rihand mismatch, just a smaller version of the same gap under a different method. The annual-mean wind-direction simplification is a real, stated limitation, not a footnote: per-overpass wind *direction* (as opposed to the per-overpass wind *speed* IME already uses, §4.2) is cached for only 18 of the 30 facilities, so this run used each facility's coarser annual mean uniformly across all 30 to keep the comparison apples-to-apples — a facility's crosswind axis rotated by the wrong day's actual wind direction plausibly degrades the fit independent of the method's other weaknesses, and this has not yet been disentangled from OCO-3's inherently sparse per-overpass sampling (per-cross-section fit R² was itself low, mostly 0.01–0.25, for most of the 10 successful fits). **IME remains the better-justified physics method for this dataset** — now on direct comparative evidence, not default choice alone. Full per-facility results, the IME-vs-Gaussian scatter, and the LOO comparison table: `data/gaussian_crosssection_results.json`, `data/gaussian_crosssection_vs_ime.png`, `WEEK15_LOG.txt`.

### 5.3 Cross-Track Experiment: Activity Signal and Reliability Modeling

As a first, deliberately narrow step toward fusing the two tracks, the already-trained Track A detector was used purely for inference (not retrained) to produce a per-facility "activity probability" and a 64-dimensional embedding for each Track B facility. This activity signal was found to broadly track the physics-based emission estimate, with one facility flagged as a notable outlier.

A follow-up experiment tested whether this activity signal — or the independent wind/CO2-offset alignment metric — could predict Track B's own self-reported relative uncertainty, via single-feature Pearson correlation and leave-one-out cross-validation. At the original N=7 facilities, the best single-feature correlation found was r = −0.62, but this did not survive leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO R² = −1.22), and the result was reported as inconclusive/negative. Re-run after the facility-set expansion at N=17 (re-running `physics_ime.py` against the full candidate set surfaced 4 more usable estimates than had previously been computed), the same single-feature-LOO-CV method now finds a real, physically sensible signal: `hit_days` (the number of per-overpass wind-matched days) correlates with the physics estimator's relative uncertainty at r=−0.617, with LOO R²=0.212 (MAE=0.095) — more wind-matched days genuinely predicts a tighter uncertainty interval. This is the first time this specific feasibility check has found anything, and illustrates a general point: a negative result reported honestly at a small sample size should be revisited, not treated as final, once more data becomes available. Consistent with the project's stated methodological discipline, still only a single-feature linear fit was attempted at this sample size, per the same underpowered-multi-feature-model caution that motivated the original scoping decision.

A related consistency check — comparing the activity signal computed from the tile-level-split versus facility-level-split detector checkpoints (§5.1) — originally found (at N=9, 4 training facilities) that facilities *never* included in the detector's training set showed substantially less stable activity-probability outputs (swings of up to 0.22) across the two checkpoints than a facility that had been in training, consistent with the facility-level-split finding that the earlier checkpoint had partly memorized specific training facilities. Re-run after the facility-set expansion at N=20 (20 facilities, 4 held out only in the facility-split checkpoint), this memorization signature disappeared: the held-out group's mean absolute delta (0.036) is now *smaller* than the in-training group's (0.061), the opposite of what a memorizing model would show — consistent with the LOFO finding above that facility-level generalization improved substantially with more training facilities, even though it remains far from perfect.

**A further follow-up (`lofo_recall_correlates.py`, N=20) asked what predicts the exhaustive LOFO recall reported in §5.1.** The strongest predictor by a wide margin is the standard model's own activity-probability confidence for that facility (r=+0.903, LOO R²=0.783, MAE=0.149); the number of OCO-3 soundings collected for that facility (an entirely different data modality, Track B rather than Track A) is a weaker secondary signal (r=+0.596). Because the activity-probability feature and the LOFO-recall target both ultimately derive from the same detector architecture and tile set, this should be read as "facilities with a clearer combustion signature generalize better," not as two fully independent measurements agreeing.

**5.3.1a A first fusion attempt without a ground-truth label.** RESEARCH_PLAN.md §9's A1→A5 Q-correcting ablation ladder needs an independent ground-truth emissions source this project does not have — Climate TRACE is explicitly barred as a training label. `track_fusion_model.py` tests what IS buildable without one: a Track A + Track B reliability/trust fusion, evaluated against — never fit to — Climate TRACE's bracketing outcome (the fusion score is computed first, from `q_rel_std` and `activity_prob_mean` alone, and only afterward compared against bracketing success). Two questions, both negative: (1) adding `activity_prob_mean` to `hit_days` in a 2-feature LOO fit predicting `q_rel_std` made the fit *worse*, not better (LOO R² 0.212→0.173) — Track A's confidence signal carries no incremental information about Track B's own uncertainty beyond what `hit_days` already captures; (2) a composite trust score (low predicted uncertainty + high activity confidence) showed essentially no separation between facilities actually bracketed by their own interval (mean trust 0.477) and those missed (0.470) — a 0.007 gap at N=17, indistinguishable from noise. This rules out one concrete, low-risk fusion approach rather than establishing that Track A and Track B cannot usefully be combined at all.

**5.3.1b Finding real ground truth and building the actual correction model.** The blocker in §5.3.1a — no independent, non-Climate-TRACE emissions source — turned out to be solvable: the **Central Electricity Authority (CEA)**, India's Ministry of Power, publishes a **CO2 Baseline Database**, freely downloadable and requiring no login. Unlike Climate TRACE, this is not satellite/thermal-proxy inference — it is a bottom-up calculation from each plant's own reported fuel consumption × gross calorific value × IPCC/national emission factor, the same accounting basis used for real Clean Development Mechanism carbon-credit determinations. This places it outside RESEARCH_PLAN.md §7's Climate-TRACE-specific restriction, making it a legitimate training/validation label. `pull_cea_ground_truth.py` downloaded FY2020-21 data (the closest single-fiscal-year match to Track B's 2020-calendar-year OCO-3 estimates; India's fiscal year is April–March) and matched all 30 candidate facilities by name — several so closely (CEA's "VINDH_CHAL STPS" vs. this project's own "VINDH_CHAL_STPS") that the original facility set plausibly derives from this same database. `q_correction_model.py` then built the first genuine Q-correcting model, originally evaluated by leave-one-out CV at N=17: baseline Track B accuracy against real ground truth was MAE 1.01 in log-ratio space (log(ours/CEA), the correct scale for multiplicative error) with a systematic underestimate bias and 47.1% bracketing (8/17) — an independently meaningful number alongside the Climate TRACE figure, since it's measured against actual reported emissions rather than another satellite-inferred estimator. At that facility count, the strongest single-feature predictor of that error was `bg_std_ppm` (background XCO2 noise, r=+0.58) — physically sensible, since a noisier background makes the near-minus-background signal less reliable. Correcting each facility's estimate by this feature's LOO-predicted bias reduced MAE to 0.902, an ~11% point-estimate improvement. **§5.3.1d reports this analysis re-run at N=24** once the 9 newly-added facilities (§5.2.1) gained both a Q estimate and an activity signal; the headline direction (a real but not-yet-significant single-feature correction) holds, but the specific best feature changes.

**5.3.1c Strengthening the correction model: a point estimate is not a complete claim.** The 1.012→0.902 MAE improvement above was, as first reported (N=17), a point estimate with no attached uncertainty — not defensible as a standalone paper claim. Three follow-up checks answer what a reviewer would ask next, each honestly regardless of outcome. First, a bootstrap 95% confidence interval on the improvement (1000 resamples over facilities): [−0.29, +0.51] — **includes zero**, meaning the improvement is not statistically distinguishable from sampling noise at N=17. Second, a leave-one-facility-out sensitivity check: the improvement stays positive in all 17 of 17 re-fits, so it is not an artifact of one influential facility — a small but directionally consistent effect, not outlier-driven, even though it falls short of significance. Third, a two-feature extension: combining `bg_std_ppm` with `n_soundings` reduces LOO MAE further, to 0.846 — a real additional lead, reported here as indicative only, consistent with this project's established caution (§7.4-derived) against multi-feature models at this facility count. The combined, honest characterization for this correction model at N=17 is: real, physically motivated, and directionally robust, but not yet statistically significant — both halves of that sentence belong in any citation of this result. **§5.3.1d re-runs this exact three-check analysis at N=24.**

**5.3.1d Extending the correction model to N=24, and a feature-ranking change reported directly.** Once the 9 newly-added facilities (§5.2.1) gained a `physics_ime.py` Q estimate (§5.2.1) and a Track A activity signal (`export_new_positive_tiles.py` + `extract_activity_signal.py`, run for the first time on these facilities), `q_correction_model.py` and `strengthen_q_correction_model.py` were re-run at the full N=24 (every facility with a Q estimate, a CEA match, and an activity signal). Baseline accuracy is essentially unchanged (MAE 1.02, bracketing 45.8% (11/24), versus 1.01/47.1% at N=17) — but the **strongest single-feature predictor of the log-ratio error changes from `bg_std_ppm` (r=+0.58 at N=17) to `hit_days`, the number of per-overpass wind-matched days (r=+0.562 at N=24)**, with `bg_std_ppm` now second (r=+0.463) and `n_soundings` close behind (r=+0.520). This is reported directly, not reconciled after the fact, in the same spirit as §5.6.2's SHAP-vs-correlation disagreement: `bg_std_ppm`'s physical rationale (a noisier background degrades the near-minus-background signal) remains sound, but at a larger, more diverse facility set, `hit_days` — a proxy for how much per-overpass wind data actually anchored the estimate — is a marginally stronger single predictor of overall estimator error, plausibly because it captures both a wind-quality effect and a general facility-sample-size effect that `bg_std_ppm` alone does not. Correcting by the LOO-predicted `hit_days` bias reduces MAE to 0.933 (an ~8.2% improvement, smaller than N=17's ~11%), and the same three robustness checks as §5.3.1c hold at N=24: a bootstrap 95% CI on the improvement of [−0.20, +0.38] **still includes zero** (not yet statistically significant, consistent with the N=17 finding), the improvement stays positive in all 24 of 24 leave-one-out re-fits (still robust to any single facility), and a two-feature extension — `hit_days` + `bg_std_ppm`, the two strongest individual predictors combined — reduces LOO MAE further to 0.894, the best correction found at either facility count. The honest summary carried forward from N=17 still holds at N=24: real, physically motivated, directionally robust, but not yet statistically significant — with the added, itself-honestly-reported caveat that *which* single feature earns that description is not stable across facility-set sizes, and should not be over-interpreted as a fixed physical law until it stabilizes over further facility-count growth.

### 5.4 Spatial Validation: A Decisive, Honestly-Reported Negative Result

**5.4.1 The plume model, at face value.** Applying §4.3's model to three initially well-bracketed, strong-signal facilities (Rihand, Talcher, Anpara) produces visually plausible, correctly-oriented plume shapes with peak ground-level concentrations spanning 4.5×10⁻⁴ to 4.3×10⁻³ kg/m³, varying roughly 2–3× across the stability-class/stack-height sensitivity grid — the ablation range that must accompany any single default-assumption number, not hidden behind it.

**5.4.2 A naive spatial-consistency test overstates its own result.** Testing whether real OCO-3 soundings within the plume's predicted downwind sector show elevated XCO2 excess relative to soundings outside it found a positive, apparently significant effect for all three prototype facilities (Pearson r=0.08–0.38; sector z=1.97–11.43). Scaled to all 18 eligible facilities, the pattern held.

**5.4.3 A permutation-based robustness check reverses this conclusion.** Two confounds could produce the §5.4.2 result without real plume-direction physics: a pure distance-from-source effect independent of true direction, or non-uniform azimuthal sampling from OCO-3's swath/orbital geometry (a phenomenon this project had already found in a different context, §5.2.5's seasonal-sampling artifact). A random-sector null baseline (2000 randomly-oriented sectors per facility, same statistical test) controls for both at once. Result: **only 1 of 14 testable facilities (Rihand, p=0.045, marginal) survives at p<0.05.** The other 13 facilities' apparent effects are statistically indistinguishable from what a randomly-oriented sector produces on the same sounding data — for one facility (Anpara), the null distribution's own standard deviation (12.5) exceeds the observed effect (10.6), a clear signature of geometry-driven noise, not a directional signal.

**5.4.4 The most likely proposed fix does not rescue the result — and clarifies it.** The aggregate-wind test compares every sounding against one facility-wide annual/overpass-averaged wind direction, discarding which specific day each sounding was taken. Fetching genuinely new per-day ERA5 wind direction data (555 days × 18 facilities) and re-testing each sounding against its own overpass-day wind, with a matched rotated-null robustness check, found **0 of 11 testable facilities** significant — worse than the aggregate-wind result, not better. Most tellingly, Rihand — the one facility that marginally survived §5.4.3 — flips to a *negative*, clearly non-significant result (z=−4.89) under day-matching, strongly suggesting its earlier marginal survival was a coincidence of the coarser aggregate wind estimate rather than a real day-level signal.

**5.4.5 Honest conclusion.** As currently built, the plume model's predicted spatial orientation does not reliably align with real OCO-3 spatial data at either wind-matching granularity tested. This does not undermine Track B's scalar estimate or its ground-truth correction (§5.3.1b–c), which do not depend on plume direction being spatially correct — it means the hotspot map should be presented strictly as a physics-consistent visualization calibrated to a validated mass flux, with its orientation explicitly flagged as unvalidated, exactly as `data/schema/emission_record_schema.json`'s `plume.validated` field does by design (§7). One remaining, untested hypothesis — true per-overpass-*time* wind (ERA5's daily collection is a daily-mean composite, not matched to each sounding's exact UTC overpass time, which varies with the ISS's precessing orbit) — was identified but not attempted in this pass, given diminishing returns after two independent negative results.

### 5.5 Temporal Results: A Verified Structural Finding, Not a Bug

Splitting each facility's 2020 soundings by calendar month (§4.4) and checking whether the mean of available monthly Q values tracks the existing annual estimate found a ratio of **0.60 ± 0.33 across 18 facilities — not near 1.0.** Rather than accept this as evidence of a coding error, it was verified analytically: IME scales roughly linearly with sounding count `n` (it sums individual soundings' excess), but the effective plume length `L_eff = √(n · footprint_area)` scales as `√n`, so `Q ∝ n/√n = √n`. A monthly subsample with fewer soundings than the full year therefore systematically yields a smaller Q than a naive annual/12 comparison would suggest, from sample-size normalization alone — confirmed against the predicted order of magnitude (`√(1/12) ≈ 0.29`, in the same direction as the observed bias). **Consequence for interpretation: absolute monthly Q values from this method are not comparable to the annual scalar, and must not be reported as literal monthly tons/year figures** — they remain meaningfully comparable to each other, within a facility, as a relative seasonal shape only, since the bias applies consistently across a given facility's own sampling pattern. A second confound affects even that relative comparison: months 7, 8, and 10 have no usable data for *any* facility in the dataset, almost certainly an OCO-3 monsoon-cloud-cover retrieval gap (June–September) rather than a genuine emissions dip — the apparent November/December peak visible in the six best-covered facilities cannot currently be disentangled from this satellite-sampling seasonality, and is not presented as a validated emissions-seasonality finding.

### 5.6 Explainability Results

**5.6.1 Grad-CAM, three facilities.** Applied to `Detector3`'s facility-split checkpoint on one representative tile each for Rihand (100% LOFO recall), and Kudgi and ShriSingajiMalwa (the two facilities with 0% LOFO recall even after the 2019-data-year fix, §5.1.1), Grad-CAM gives a result consistent with, and complementary to, `diagnose_lofo_weak_facilities.py`'s earlier raw-intensity finding. Rihand's tile is classified confidently correct (P(plant)=0.997), with attribution concentrated on NO2 (67% channel share, SO2 32%, VIIRS 1%) — consistent with NO2 being the base channel chosen for its direct combustion link (§4.1). Both Kudgi (P(plant)=0.183) and ShriSingajiMalwa (P(plant)=0.153) are correctly predicted as *not confidently plant-like* by this checkpoint, with attribution split roughly evenly between NO2 and SO2 (49–50% each) and VIIRS contributing about 1% in all three cases. This is a second, independent line of evidence for the same conclusion §5.1.1 reached from raw tile intensity: the detector is not confused or attending to the wrong location for these two facilities, it genuinely does not find a strong signal in any channel — consistent with a real satellite-observability limit rather than a fixable modeling gap. Full per-facility values in `data/gradcam_3channel_summary.json`; scope is one diagnostic tile per facility, not an exhaustive per-tile study.

**5.6.2 SHAP, CEA correction model.** The 8-feature linear model (full-data fit, R²=0.720) SHAP explains produces a global feature ranking that **disagrees with the single-feature Pearson-correlation ranking already reported in §5.3.1b–c**, and this discrepancy is reported directly rather than reconciled after the fact. `bg_std_ppm` was the strongest single-feature correlate with the correction target (r=+0.58) and the best individual feature in the two-feature LOO-MAE test (§5.3.1c). Under SHAP's multivariate attribution, `capacity_mw` (mean |SHAP|=0.575) and `n_soundings` (0.559) rank above `bg_std_ppm` (0.298), with `activity_prob_std` (0.363) also ranking higher. This is not necessarily a contradiction: SHAP values reflect a feature's contribution within a fitted multivariate model, where correlated or interacting features can redistribute attributed importance in ways a univariate correlation cannot capture — a facility's plant capacity and its OCO-3 sounding count are plausibly related to how reliable its raw estimate is, independent of background noise specifically. The honest reading is that `bg_std_ppm`'s single-feature correlation and physical interpretation (§5.3.1b) remain valid on their own terms, but a full multivariate attribution suggests capacity and sounding count carry comparable or greater explanatory weight once considered jointly — worth investigating with more facilities before treating either ranking as final. Full ranking and per-facility SHAP values in `data/shap_correction_model_results.json`.

## 6. Discussion

Seven findings in this work carry implications beyond the specific numbers reported.

**First, evaluation methodology materially changes the apparent quality of a satellite-tile classifier — and this is true at two separate levels, not just one.** The 81.2%→67.3% accuracy drop under a facility-level split (with 4 facilities) — and the more dramatic 53%→8% collapse in plant recall — demonstrated that tile-level evaluation was measuring the wrong thing. But the facility-set expansion revealed a second, subtler version of the same problem: even a methodologically correct facility-level split can mislead when the facility count is too small for one random draw of held-out sites to be representative. The single-split recall of 88% (at 20 training facilities) looked like a clean resolution of the earlier collapse; only the exhaustive 21-fold LOFO evaluation exposed that this figure depended on which facilities happened to be excluded, and that true recall (47.2%) is roughly half of it. Any satellite-imagery classification task with a small-to-moderate number of distinct positive-class sites is at risk of both failure modes unless the split is constructed at the facility level *and*, ideally, evaluated exhaustively rather than on a single held-out draw.

**Second, wind measurement error, not the CO2 signal itself, is generally the dominant source of uncertainty in this class of mass-balance estimate.** This project's empirical finding (wind term contributing 45–59% relative uncertainty, versus single-digit percentages for IME sampling) independently reproduces a pattern already noted in the literature (§2.1), and suggests that further improving the accuracy of per-plant emission estimates from OCO-3-class data may depend more on improving wind data quality or matching methodology than on refining the CO2 mass-balance calculation itself.

**Third, agreement (or disagreement) with Climate TRACE should be interpreted carefully, and larger samples — or bug fixes — should be expected to look less flattering, not more.** Because Climate TRACE is itself substantially inferred from satellite thermal-proxy methods rather than measured emissions, the within-interval result is evidence of rough — but far from complete — mutual consistency between two independent, imperfect estimators, not validation against ground truth. §5.2.4's bracketing rate moved from 71% (N=7) through 53% and 35.3% (N=17) to its current 37.5% (N=24) — each step a genuinely more honest number, not a regression, and the final step small enough to read as noise around a stable rate rather than a genuine improvement. The disagreements found (both over- and under-estimates) are genuinely informative precisely because they cannot be resolved simply by trusting one source over the other; the Talcher case study (§5.2.3) shows that at least one such disagreement had an identifiable, fixable root cause (background-definition sensitivity) rather than being unexplainable noise.

**Fourth, an honestly-reported negative result at a small sample size is a snapshot, not a conclusion.** The activity-signal-predicts-uncertainty feasibility check (§5.3) was correctly reported as inconclusive at N=7 — but re-running the identical method at N=17 (after more facilities became available) found a real, physically sensible effect (`hit_days` predicting `q_rel_std`, LOO R²=0.212) that the smaller sample simply couldn't detect. The lesson generalizes beyond this specific check: in a facility-count-limited project like this one, "no effect found" and "no effect exists" are not the same claim, and results scoped down for sample-size reasons are worth revisiting whenever the facility count grows rather than treating the original negative finding as final.

**Fifth, a plausible-looking, physically-grounded spatial model can fail validation even when its calibrating scalar is reasonable — and only a permutation-based test, not a raw significance test, can tell the difference.** §5.4's plume model produced visually correct, properly-cited physics, and an initial spatial-consistency test even found "significant" results at all three (then eighteen) facilities tested. Only a random-sector null baseline revealed those effects were statistically indistinguishable from what satellite-sampling geometry alone would produce. This is a more general lesson than this project's specific facilities: any claim of spatial or directional structure in sparse, non-uniformly-sampled satellite data needs a permutation/randomization null, not just a p-value from the obvious test, and this project's own experience shows the two can disagree completely (11.4σ apparent significance, reduced to indistinguishable-from-noise).

**Sixth, a correction model's point estimate is not a complete result without its confidence interval, even when the point estimate is real.** §5.3.1c's bootstrap analysis found the CEA-ground-truth correction's ~11% MAE improvement has a 95% CI spanning zero, despite being positive in every leave-one-out re-fit — both facts describe the same underlying effect, and reporting only the point estimate (as this paper's own earlier draft did, before §5.3.1c) would have overstated its current strength.

**Seventh, an individual facility can score average-or-better on every candidate explanatory metric available and still fail decisively.** §5.2.7's four-explanation elimination on Rihand (overpass density, SNR, background-definition sensitivity, wind-match quality) found no single measurable cause, despite Rihand outperforming the 30-plant median on all four. This is a distinct lesson from the fourth finding above: it is not that the sample was too small to detect an effect, but that no effect among those tested exists at a scale that matters — a genuine ceiling on single-cause diagnosis, with direct implications for any future correction model trained on these same diagnostics, since none of them would reliably flag Rihand as untrustworthy.

The negative-CO2-enhancement anomaly (§5.2.5) has since been fully resolved: 2 of 3 affected facilities turned out to be a signal-to-noise problem (not a real negative signal or a pipeline bug), the apparent wind-alignment contradiction dissolved once wind alignment and CO2 detectability were recognized as independent diagnostics, and the third (ShriSingajiMalwa) turned out to be a seasonal-sampling-imbalance artifact that flips sign once corrected for. None of the three facilities represents a genuinely negative CO2 signal. The one durable lesson from this chain of diagnosis is methodological rather than facility-specific: `physics_ime.py`'s near/background comparison does not currently stratify by month, and this project's own data shows that gap can silently manufacture a spurious 4x-larger-than-expected "negative enhancement" when near-plant and background sampling happen to be seasonally imbalanced — worth hardening generally, not just explaining case by case.

## 7. Limitations

- **Climate TRACE is a benchmark, not ground truth**, for the reasons discussed in §2.2 and §6; agreement or disagreement with it should not be read as proof of correctness or error in either direction.
- **The cross-track reliability experiment (§5.3) is still a single-feature linear fit at N=17**, and while it now shows a real effect (up from an underpowered N=7 negative result), it remains indicative rather than a validated multi-feature model, per `RESEARCH_PLAN.md` §8's caution against fitting one at this facility count.
- **Track A's exhaustive LOFO recall, initially 47.2%, rose to 69.1% once all facilities were matched to the original top-5's 24-tile temporal depth** (§5.1.1) — a single-split recall figure should not be quoted as a stand-alone generalization claim without this context, since this project's own experience shows a single split can differ from the true rate by a factor of ~2, and shows generalization at a given data depth can itself substantially understate what's achievable with more temporal coverage. Two facilities (Kudgi, ShriSingajiMalwa) still generalize essentially not at all (0% recall) even after the fix, corroborated independently by the Grad-CAM diagnostic in §5.6.1.
- **The IME method's 2.25 km² per-sounding footprint-area assumption is fixed, not measured per sounding**, and its sensitivity has not been separately ablated in this work — Not documented / needs verification whether this materially affects the reported estimates.
- **Three facilities (Mundra, Sipat, Simhadri) have no Track B emission estimate at all**, excluded for a genuine 0-near-sounding OCO-3 coverage gap rather than a processing failure — the 17-facility Climate TRACE comparison and reliability model necessarily omit these three.
- **9 additional candidate facilities (Lalitpur, Akaltara, Bellary, Dadri(Nctpp), KGudemNew, Pryagraj(Bara), Raichur, RayalSeema, Sagardighi) now have raw OCO-3 soundings processed (§5.2.1), completing coverage of the full 30-facility candidate set; 6 of the 9 now have a `physics_ime.py` Q-estimate (§5.2.1); the Climate TRACE benchmark now covers those 6 as well (§5.2.4, N=17→24, 35.3%→37.5%); and the CEA ground-truth correction model now covers them too (§5.3.1d, N=17→24) — including a Track A activity signal, computed for the first time for these 9 (`export_new_positive_tiles.py` + `extract_activity_signal.py`)** — but Track A's LOFO evaluation and the spatial/temporal extensions have not been re-run against these facilities, and still reflect the earlier facility subset (N=18–22 depending on section); their headline numbers should not be read as covering the full 30-facility candidate list until re-run.
- **Rihand's +134% Climate TRACE mismatch remains unexplained** (§5.2.7): overpass density, SNR, background-definition sensitivity, and wind-match quality were each tested and each ruled out — Rihand scores average-or-better than the 30-plant median on all four despite the large error. Untested: plume geometry/plant layout, nearby confounding CO2 sources near Rihand's near-plant/background zones, and a possible FY2020-21-CEA-vs-2020-satellite emissions-year mismatch specific to Rihand.
- **All three originally-flagged negative-CO2-enhancement facilities are resolved** (§5.2.5): two as signal-to-noise limitations, one (ShriSingajiMalwa) as a seasonal near/background sampling-imbalance artifact. The general gap this surfaced — `physics_ime.py` does not stratify near/background comparisons by month — is not yet fixed and could recur for other facilities with similarly uneven seasonal sounding coverage.
- **CI covers only the fast unit-test suite, not integration paths.** A dependency manifest (`requirements.txt`) was added, pinned to the working conda environment — writing it surfaced an unrelated reproducibility gap: scripts have also been run under a second, separate Python installation missing several required packages, undetected until now because the scripts run most recently happened not to need those specific packages. A 37-test `unittest` suite (`tests/`) covers the deterministic math and data-pairing logic most likely to silently produce a wrong number, and now runs automatically on every push/PR via `.github/workflows/tests.yml` — but it still doesn't cover integration paths (Earth Engine, OCO-3 downloads, model training end-to-end), which remain manual.
- **A roughly four-week gap in project activity (mid-July to mid-August, per the git history) is unexplained** in any document found during this synthesis — Not documented / needs verification whether relevant work occurred elsewhere during this period.
- **The exact ERA5 wind-data access method was not independently re-confirmed** during this synthesis pass — Not documented / needs verification.
- **The plume/hotspot model's predicted spatial orientation is not validated against real data** (§5.4): only 1 of 14 testable facilities shows an effect distinguishable from a random-sector null baseline, and this survives only marginally (p=0.045). Per-overpass day-matched wind, the most likely proposed fix, was tested and made the result worse, not better. The map must be presented as a physics-consistent visualization calibrated to a validated mass flux, never as a validated spatial prediction — the machine-readable export schema (`data/schema/emission_record_schema.json`) enforces this at the data level, fixing `plume.validated` to `false`.
- **The CEA ground-truth correction's improvement is not statistically significant at either facility count tested** (§5.3.1c: N=17, bootstrap 95% CI [−0.29, +0.51]; §5.3.1d: N=24, CI [−0.20, +0.38]), despite being directionally consistent across every leave-one-out re-fit at both. **The best single corrective feature is not stable between the two facility counts** (`bg_std_ppm` at N=17, `hit_days` at N=24) — a two-feature extension combining both shows further promise at N=24 (LOO MAE 0.894) but is explicitly indicative, not a recommended production model, at this facility count.
- **Monthly Q estimates (§5.5) are not comparable in absolute terms to the annual estimate**, due to a verified structural property of the IME method's √n-scaling effective plume length — only relative within-facility seasonal comparisons are meaningful, and even those are confounded by an apparent OCO-3 monsoon-season sampling gap (no facility has usable data in months 7, 8, or 10) that this work does not disentangle from genuine seasonality.
- **The SHAP correction-model attribution (§5.6.2) is a full-data linear fit, not a leave-one-out cross-validated model** — it explains a fixed model's own predictions, which is a distinct claim from generalization, and its multivariate feature ranking disagrees with the project's earlier single-feature correlation ranking (capacity/sounding-count vs. background noise), a discrepancy reported rather than resolved. Neither ranking should be treated as final at N=17.
- **The Grad-CAM 3-channel diagnostic (§5.6.1) covers one tile per facility for three facilities**, not an exhaustive per-tile or per-facility study — it is offered as converging, independent evidence alongside `diagnose_lofo_weak_facilities.py`'s raw-intensity finding, not a standalone claim.
- **True per-overpass-time wind matching for the spatial validation was not attempted.** ERA5's daily collection is a daily-mean composite, not matched to each sounding's exact UTC overpass time (which varies with the ISS's precessing orbit) — this remains the one methodologically distinct, untested hypothesis before concluding the plume-direction claim is unsupported by available data generally, rather than just by the two wind-matching approaches tried.

## 8. Conclusion and Future Work

This project demonstrates a working, checkpointed, uncertainty-aware pipeline for estimating per-plant CO2 emissions from OCO-3 satellite data for Indian coal power plants, validated against a genuine, non-satellite ground-truth source (India's CEA CO2 Baseline Database) — the paper's strongest and safest empirical claim, reported with its confidence interval rather than only its point estimate. It separately demonstrates a satellite-tile power-plant detector fused across three physically distinct channels, with an exhaustive leave-one-facility-out evaluation resolving two successive rounds of evaluation-methodology overstatement (tile-level, then single-split facility-level). A spatial extension (a wind-conditioned Gaussian plume model) and a temporal extension (monthly Q estimates) were built, both to a genuine research gap neither Climate TRACE nor this project's cited baseline appear to fill — and both honestly report a negative or structurally-caveated result under rigorous validation, which is itself a methodological contribution (a permutation-test discipline for spatial claims against satellite sounding data, and a verified structural finding about sub-annual IME estimation) rather than a failure of the overall approach. Two further benchmarking passes, added after the above was first written up, keep the paper's own honesty discipline pointed at itself: a capacity-only baseline beats the satellite estimate outright on predicting CEA ground truth (§5.2.1b), and a second physics-based Q estimator benchmarked directly against IME fits fewer facilities and predicts CEA worse wherever comparable (§5.2.8) — both are reported as genuine limits on the satellite approach's current standing, not smoothed into the surrounding narrative. A regional coverage map (§3.1) separately quantifies just how sparse the underlying OCO-3 data is outside each facility's own sampling footprint, context for reading every Track B number in this paper.

## 9. A Segmentation-Only Deep Learning Extension

### 9.1 Why segmentation only, not the blueprint's full architecture

The project's original deep-learning blueprint (`4ypblueprint.pdf`, Paper 2 / Dumont Le Brazidec et al. 2024) proposes a two-stage architecture for satellite CO2 point-source imagery: a U-Net segmentation stage that localizes the plume within a tile, feeding a CNN regression stage that predicts the point source's emission rate Q. Track B, as built in this project, never implemented either stage — it remained a pure physics pipeline (`physics_ime.py`, §4.2).

Building this architecture requires labeled (tile, mask, Q) training triples, and no real labeled dataset of this kind exists at this project's scale — the same constraint SMARTCARB addressed with a transport-model simulator rather than real labels. `simulate_training_pairs.py` was built for exactly this purpose, reusing this project's own validated Gaussian plume physics (`plume_model.py`, §4.3) as its simulation engine.

An eight-task investigation (Week 20; full detail in `WEEK20_LOG.txt` and `SIMULATOR_METHODOLOGY_NOTE.md`) attempted to validate the simulator's Q labels against this project's own real-world Q-accuracy standard (`physics_ime.py`'s known accuracy against CEA ground truth, §5.2.1). Each task was mechanistically distinct — near-field dispersion physics, tile resolution matching, per-pixel area-averaging, an IME-consistent spatial readout, multi-day sounding pooling, real OCO-3 orbital sampling geometry (Snapshot Area Mapping), per-facility sounding-density calibration, and finally a closed-form statistical correction for a diagnosed noise-floor bias. The investigation concluded that simulated Q labels could not be brought to this project's trusted accuracy standard: the blocking mechanism, isolated by the final two tasks, is that individual-sounding signal-to-noise makes accurate Q recovery from sparse, noisy simulated samples inherently unstable — a property of the underlying measurement/estimation problem, not a fixable simulator defect. That investigation explicitly distinguished this from the plume **mask**, which is built directly from the true, noise-free physics field and was never part of the failed Q-calibration pipeline. Q regression, as the blueprint scoped it, is therefore treated here as a **documented deviation**, not attempted in this section. This section builds and evaluates only the segmentation stage.

### 9.2 Data and model

Training data: `simulate_training_pairs.py`'s simulated tiles (`data/simulated_train/simulated_tiles.npz`) — 2,223 64×64 single-channel XCO2 tiles (1,923 positive/plume, 300 negative), each with a binary ground-truth plume mask. `q_t_per_year` is loaded only to bucket test tiles by true emission strength for reporting; it is never used as a training target.

**Coverage/cloud-gap masking** (as of Week 22 — see §9.5 for why this changed from the originally-reported version): each tile's valid/invalid pixel mask is generated by reusing Week 20 Task 6's real, already-validated OCO-3 SAM-mode sparse footprint sampler, restricted to the tile's 60km extent, rather than an earlier random-rectangular-block approximation. Resulting coverage is sparse (median 3.9% of pixels valid, mean 5.5%, up to ~15% for the best-observed facilities) — matched in density to real single-overpass OCO-3 coverage (11–13%, §9.4), though §9.5 reports that matching density alone did not resolve the transfer problem it was meant to fix.

Split discipline matches this project's own established rule (§5.1, Track A's Week 11 facility-level-split fix): tiles are split by `facility_id`, not individually — multiple tiles can share a synthetic facility (different simulated overpass days/wind directions for the same plant), and a tile-level split would leak. 70%/15%/15% train/val/test by facility unit (350/75/75 of 500 total units: 200 simulated positive facilities plus 300 independently-sampled negative tiles, each its own unit), giving 1,625/317/281 tiles respectively. Zero facility overlap between splits is asserted in code, not just assumed by construction.

Cloud-gap NaN pixels are excluded from both the loss and every reported metric via a validity mask (verified directly: an extreme out-of-range prediction at an excluded pixel leaves the loss numerically unchanged) — chosen over adding a second input channel as the simpler of the two options, since the ground-truth mask is defined everywhere but the network has no evidence at an occluded pixel. (§9.5 flags, but does not confirm, that this NaN-fill approach may itself be part of the unresolved real-tile transfer problem.)

Model: a compact 3-level U-Net (488K parameters, `unet_segmentation.py`), sized for this dataset and for training on a MacBook Air M1 (`mps` backend, no CUDA) rather than a full 5-level architecture. Loss combines pixel-wise BCE with a soft Dice term, both restricted to valid pixels — Dice is included because plume masks are sparse (mean 4.6% of pixels positive on positive tiles).

### 9.3 Results

Evaluated on the facility-held-out test split (281 tiles / 75 facilities, never seen during training or checkpoint selection), current (Week 22) model:

| | Mean | Median | N |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positive-tile Dice | 0.371 | 0.286 | 231 |
| Positive-tile IoU | 0.308 | 0.167 | 231 |
| Negative-tile Dice | 0.780 | 1.000 | 50 |

The model reliably predicts "no plume" on negative tiles (median Dice 1.0) but achieves only modest overlap on positive tiles at this coverage density, with a heavy tail of near-total failures pulling the mean below the median. Splitting positive test tiles into the bottom tercile by true Q ("low-Q", ≤6.18×10⁶ t/yr, n=86) versus the rest ("high-Q", n=145):

| | Low-Q median Dice | High-Q median Dice |
|---|---|---|
| Dice | 0.038 | 0.364 |

Performance degrades sharply on weak-signal tiles — the same underlying signal-to-noise limitation §9.1's Q-calibration investigation diagnosed shows up here as a harder segmentation problem for weak plumes, not only a harder scalar-recovery problem. These numbers are noticeably lower than an earlier version of this model trained on denser (but less realistic) simulated coverage — §9.5 reports that comparison explicitly and explains why: this is a genuinely harder segmentation problem at realistic coverage density, not a regression in the training procedure. Qualitative inspection (`data/unet_segmentation_qualitative.png`, six facility-held-out test tiles selected by a fixed random seed, not cherry-picked) shows a correspondingly sparse picture, consistent with the much lower input coverage. Validation Dice across training epochs was noisy (not a clean monotone convergence) — reported as observed, not smoothed over. Full detail is in `WEEK21_LOG.txt` (original version) and `WEEK22_LOG.txt` (current version).

### 9.4 What this result does and does not establish

This is a **simulated-data-only capability demonstration**. It shows that a compact U-Net can learn a moderate-quality plume-localization signal from this project's physics-based simulator, with an interpretable failure mode (weak-signal tiles) rather than an opaque one. It does **not** show that this model — or this architecture — generalizes to real OCO-3 satellite tiles: the simulator's own validated limitations (§9.1; `SIMULATOR_METHODOLOGY_NOTE.md`) mean its XCO2 fields, while built from validated dispersion physics, are not a substitute for real satellite imagery, and no real-tile evaluation has been attempted. The Dice/IoU numbers above characterize this model's performance against this project's own simulated ground truth, not against a real-world benchmark. Extending this capability to real tiles, and revisiting Q regression under a materially different approach (§6 of `SIMULATOR_METHODOLOGY_NOTE.md`), remain open, explicitly out of scope for this section.

An **illustrative-only** eyeball check (not a validation — no real ground-truth mask exists to score against) ran the trained model on real single-overpass-day OCO-3 tiles for Vindhyachal and Rihand, gridded onto this project's standard 60km/64px convention (`sanity_check_real_tiles.py`; full detail in `WEEK21_LOG.txt`'s qualitative addendum). Two things stood out, purely as visual observations: real single-day coverage (11–13% of the tile) is far sparser than anything in training (45–85% valid), and the predicted "plume" regions for both plants closely tracked the real data's own scattered coverage-speckle pattern rather than resembling a coherent plume shape. This is consistent with, not contradictory to, this section's existing conclusion that the model is not validated against real tiles — it does not add a validation result, only a concrete illustration of the same limitation.

### 9.5 Attempting to close the coverage-geometry gap (Week 22) — density fixed, coverage-tracking behavior NOT resolved

§9.4's real-tile check diagnosed a specific, checkable cause: training tiles' cloud-gap mask (large random rectangular blocks, 45–85% valid) looked nothing like real single-overpass coverage (sparse scattered speckle, 11–13% valid). Week 22 fixed this directly, reusing — not reinventing — machinery this project had already validated: Week 20 Task 6's real OCO-3 SAM-mode sparse footprint sampler (itself verified against real per-facility sounding counts, within 1% for all validated facilities) now generates each training tile's valid/cloud-gap mask, restricted to the tile's 60km extent, with the same per-facility retention characteristic used for that facility's Q-readout SAM scan.

**Confirmed before doing anything else**: the fix changed the density as intended — valid-pixel fraction across the retrained training set now has median 3.9%, mean 5.5%, p90 12.9%, max 14.9% (down from the old uniform 45–85% range), with the real reference points (11–13%) landing near the top of this distribution, consistent with Vindhyachal and Rihand being among the best-observed real facilities rather than typical ones.

The model was retrained on this corrected data and re-evaluated identically (facility-held-out test split, same metrics):

| | Week 21 (old coverage) | Week 22 (SAM coverage) |
|---|---|---|
| Positive-tile median Dice | 0.727 | 0.286 |
| Positive-tile median IoU | 0.571 | 0.167 |
| Negative-tile median Dice | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| Low-Q median Dice | 0.380 | 0.038 |
| High-Q median Dice | 0.846 | 0.364 |

Simulated-data metrics got **worse**, not better — expected and explained, not a bug: median input coverage dropped roughly 15–20x, making this an intrinsically harder segmentation problem, independent of whether the fix addressed the real underlying issue.

**The real test — re-running the qualitative real-tile check with the retrained model — is decisive, and it is a second negative result.** The predicted "plume" pixels for both plants again cluster in a shape closely resembling the real data's own coverage-speckle pattern, not a coherent plume — visually unchanged from §9.4's finding despite density now being matched. **This is reported plainly, not smoothed into a claim of progress on the simulated-Dice numbers**: coverage *density* was not the (or not the only) cause of the coverage-tracking behavior. Diagnosed, not left as a mystery: the model appears to associate the *shape* of the valid/invalid pixel boundary with "plume," largely independent of coverage density — candidates include a NaN-fill-boundary shortcut (a sharp discontinuity at every coverage edge, trivially detectable regardless of density) and this simulator's coverage pattern still being structurally different from real speckle even at matched density (no simulated satellite heading, a standing simplification since Task 6). Neither was confirmed this session.

Two independent attempts at making single-snapshot simulated tiles behave like real satellite data (Week 20 Task 5's multi-day pooling, and this week's coverage-geometry fix) have now both fallen short in ways that point toward the underlying **simulation methodology**, not a single tunable parameter. One further, more targeted attempt was made in a follow-up session before concluding this line of investigation — see §9.6.

### 9.6 A third, final attempt (Week 23) — confirmed structural finding, not another open question

§9.5 left two untested candidates for the coverage-tracking behavior: coverage-mask *shape* still differing from real speckle even at matched density, or the model exploiting the NaN-fill value's boundary discontinuity as an implicit shortcut feature. Week 23 tested the second directly: an explicit second input channel (1=valid, 0=missing), so the model no longer has to infer validity from a fill-value discontinuity. **Consistency was verified, not assumed**: both the simulated-training and real-tile pipelines build this channel by calling one shared function (`unet_segmentation.build_model_input`) with no independently-written second implementation anywhere in the codebase — the only way two pipelines are guaranteed identical construction.

Simulated-data metrics were essentially flat versus §9.5's version (positive-tile median Dice/IoU identical to three decimal places, 0.286/0.167 both versions). **The real-tile check — the decisive test — is a third negative result**: predicted "plume" pixels for both plants again cluster in the same coverage-speckle-tracking shape, visually nearly indistinguishable from §9.5's figure. Giving the model an explicit valid-mask channel did not change this behavior.

**This is reported as a confirmed structural finding, not left as another open question.** Across three mechanistically distinct, honestly-reported attempts (baseline diagnosis, §9.4; coverage density, §9.5; coverage-shape disambiguation via an explicit channel, §9.6), this project's simulated training data has not been made to produce a segmentation model that transfers to real single-overpass OCO-3 tiles. Neither the density hypothesis nor the NaN-fill-boundary shortcut hypothesis explains or fixes the behavior. Per this project's diminishing-returns discipline (applied identically in `WEEK20_LOG.txt`'s Q-recovery investigation), a fourth variant was not attempted. `SIMULATOR_METHODOLOGY_NOTE.md` §6.1 now records a fundamentally different label-generation methodology as the **sole remaining untried path** for real-tile transfer, not one of several candidates. Full detail, including what remains genuinely untested (e.g. whether the coverage pattern is confirmed as the dominant learned shortcut versus some other simulator-specific artifact), is in `WEEK21_LOG.txt`, `WEEK22_LOG.txt`, and `WEEK23_LOG.txt`.
